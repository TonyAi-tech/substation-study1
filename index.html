Lesson 1

The Study Loop

Run this same six-step cycle on every lesson. It's the whole method.

  1. Read the intro — get the big picture before anything else.
  2. Check the learning objectives — that's your target for the lesson.
  3. Skim the lesson questions — now you know what to look for while reading.
  4. Read the references carefully — if you can't explain it in your own words, you don't know it yet. Underline key points.
  5. Answer the questions — go back to the material on anything you're unsure of.
  6. Review before class — be ready to answer out loud.
A little every day beats cramming. And if you don't put in the effort, the Committee can remove you from the program.

Responsibility

Show up, meet deadlines, follow through on what you agreed to when you signed the contract.

You represent the trade well and work well with the crew.

Lesson 4

Your Job & the
Future of the Trade

Why the work is there

  • Construction is a major driver of the U.S. economy — when the economy grows, construction grows with it.
  • There's a real skilled worker shortage. Qualified electricians are in demand.
  • Contractors won't hire someone who just claims to be an electrician — you're already ahead by committing to the apprenticeship.

Where it's headed

  • Electrical work is no longer just lighting — it's fiber optics, solar, and smart buildings.
  • Skilled workers can move into supervisory roles or become contractors themselves.
  • Success = technical skill plus being productive, dependable, and good on a team.
Contractors rely on organizations like the IBEW to make sure they have access to skilled workers who keep them profitable. Your skill and attitude shape the future of the trade.
Lesson 5

Sexual Harassment

What the law says

  • Violates Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
  • Two legal categories: quid pro quo and hostile work environment.
  • It doesn't have to be sexual in nature — unfair job assignments based on gender count too.
  • The EEOC reviews the whole record and judges each case on its own facts.

What you do

  • If it's unwelcome and the recipient finds it offensive, it's harassment — intent doesn't decide it.
  • Victim or witness? Report it to the proper company official immediately.
  • Confidentiality matters — you may end up a witness.
  • Watch what you say and what goes on your hard hat.
Prevention is the best tool. You represent your apprenticeship, the IBEW, your contractor, and the utility. Apprentices have been dismissed for this.

Don't mix these up

Who Does What

IBEW

The union — represents the workers. Founded 1891. Organizes, bargains, and protects members.

NECA

Represents the contractors. Founded 1901. Sets standards, lobbies, and promotes the industry. Organized into 9 geographic districts plus 1 district for outside line construction.

CIR

Council on Industrial Relations, formed 1920. The referee between IBEW and NECA — resolves disputes by arbitration instead of strikes.

NEBF

National Electrical Benefit Fund, 1946. Retirement benefits funded by employer contributions — a percentage of gross payroll. HQ in Bethesda, MD.

NJAC / NJATC

The joint training committee, formed 1941 — the origin of national apprenticeship standards.

Lesson 8

The Four Stages of
Alcohol & Drug Use

  1. Experimentation — trying it, usually socially. No obvious behavioral signs yet.
  2. Regular Use — more frequent. Friends change, school or work performance starts slipping.
  3. Preoccupation & Daily Use — the drug becomes the focus. Motivation disappears; the desire for it takes over.
  4. Dependence & Severe Effects — physical and mental deterioration. Depression, family and legal trouble, blackouts, possible overdose.

Warning signs

  • Mood swings, unexplained weight loss, bloodshot eyes
  • Declining performance at work or school
  • Frequent intoxication, driving under the influence
  • Using alcohol to cope with problems

How to help

  • Acknowledging the issue is the first step.
  • Be understanding, firm, and supportive — not critical or judgmental.
  • Offer help finding treatment without enabling the behavior.
  • Groups like Al-Anon support families and connect you with local resources.

Lesson 10

The IBEW Constitution

Core objectives

  • Assist others in sickness and distress
  • Cultivate friendship among those in the industry
  • Organize all workers in the entire electrical industry
  • Secure adequate pay
  • Secure employment
  • Seek security for the individual

Rules worth memorizing

  • Local Union meetings must adjourn by 11:00 PM.
  • A Local can meet twice a month without declaring a special call.
  • Members owing 6 months' dues aren't eligible for pension and death benefits.
  • Owe 3 months of dues and you risk being suspended from membership.
  • To hold office you need 5 years of continuous good standing.
  • Nominations for officers are held in May of election years.
Dues are divided between the General Fund and the Pension Fund. To be a good union member, be an informed one — take part in your Local's activities.

Professional Conduct

A Qualified Electrical Worker (QEW) is held to the same standard of conduct and responsibility as a doctor or a lawyer.

Traits of a professional

  • Responsible, timely, reliable, proactive
  • Honest and trustworthy — shows integrity
  • Service-minded and team-oriented
  • Communicates well and respects authority
  • Maintains confidentiality
  • Presents a positive image of the employer

Benefits of IBEW membership

  • Better training and higher wages
  • Better benefits and more job security
  • Greater democracy in the workplace
  • Better health and safety
  • Collective bargaining
  • Greater equity in pay
Professionalism starts in the apprenticeship and continues your whole career. The first step is recognizing your role in the organization and how it affects the company.

What actually causes it

PART 02

Electricity
Fundamentals

What electricity actually is, the four ways we make it, how batteries work, and what makes a circuit complete.

Fundamentals

What Electricity Is

Electricity is a form of energy created by the movement of electrons — tiny charged particles — through a conductor like a copper wire.

Static Electricity

Charge builds up on a surface and stays put. It's an imbalance of charge — like your hair standing up after you take off a hat.

Current Electricity

The continuous flow of electrons through a conductor. This is what powers your tools, devices, and buildings.

DC vs. AC

DC — Direct CurrentElectrons flow in one direction only.
AC — Alternating CurrentElectrons reverse direction periodically.
Fundamentals

Four Ways We
Make Electricity

Magnetism

A magnet creates an invisible field that exerts force on magnetic materials like iron. The foundation for everything below it.

Electromagnetic Induction

Move a conductor through a magnetic field — or spin a magnetic field near a coil — and electrons are pushed into motion. This is how power plants work: steam, wind, or water spins a turbine near magnets. Faster spin = more electricity.

Thermoelectricity

Heat one side of a special material and keep the other cool. The temperature difference drives electrons from hot to cold, creating current. Used in portable coolers and remote generators.

Piezoelectricity

Squeeze or stretch certain crystals like quartz and they produce a charge. Think of squeezing a sponge — except electricity comes out instead of water. Example: the spark when you click a lighter.

Fundamentals

How a Battery Works

A battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a chemical reaction. An electrolyte battery and a lead-acid battery are the same thing.

  1. Anode (negative side) — releases electrons.
  2. Cathode (positive side) — accepts electrons.
  3. Electrolyte — a liquid or paste that lets charged particles (ions) move between anode and cathode.
  4. Connect it to a circuit and the reaction at the anode releases electrons, which flow through the wire to the cathode. That flow is the current.
Connect a copper wire to a battery and the electric field pushes free electrons away from the negative terminal and pulls them toward the positive. Electrons hop atom to atom — that's the flow of charge we call electricity. Heat is produced along the way from the current flowing through the conductor.
Fundamentals

Secondary Cells

The only difference that matters: can it be recharged?

TypeRechargeableUsed forExamples

Fundamentals

Circuits & Switches

Complete circuit

  • A complete circuit lets current flow to the load and back to the source.
  • A schematic is the electrical drawing that shows that path — you'll use them constantly.
  • As an incandescent lamp's filament resistance increases, its wattage rating decreases. Lamps use poor conductors that get hot and glow red or white hot.

Switches

  • A switch is a mechanical device that allows or interrupts current flow.
  • Closed = ON. The circuit is complete and current flows.
  • Open = OFF. The path is broken — this is an open circuit.
  • Switches are rated by maximum current and maximum voltage.
Test trap: The location of the switch in the circuit doesn't matter. All it has to do is separate the terminals of the source so electrons can't flow.

PART 05

Series vs.
Parallel

Two circuit types, five laws each. Get the difference locked in and half the test math gets easier.

One path

Laws of Series Circuits

  1. Has only ONE path for current to flow.
  2. Total source voltage equals the sum of each load voltage.  ET = E₁ + E₂ + E₃
  3. Current stays the same throughout.  IT = I₁ = I₂ = I₃
  4. Total resistance equals the sum of each load resistance.  RT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃
  5. Total power equals the sum of each load's power.  PT = P₁ + P₂ + P₃

Multiple paths

Laws of Parallel Circuits

  1. Has MULTIPLE paths for current to flow.
  2. Voltage stays the same throughout.  ET = E₁ = E₂ = E₃
  3. Total source current equals the sum of each load current.  IT = I₁ + I₂ + I₃
  4. Total resistance is always less than the smallest resistor.
  5. Total power equals the sum of each load's power.  PT = P₁ + P₂ + P₃
RTotal = 1 ÷ (1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃)

Lock it in

The One-Line
Difference

Series

Amps stay the SAME.
Everything else adds up.

One path means every electron passes through every load — so the current can't change. Each load takes a share of the voltage.

Parallel

Volts stay the SAME.
Everything else adds up — except resistance, which shrinks.

Every branch connects to the same two points, so every branch sees full voltage. More branches = more paths = less total resistance.

Power always adds in both circuit types. If you only remember one thing, remember which quantity stays constant — the rest follows.
PART 06

Wire Sizing &
Voltage Drop

AWG, Kcmil, circular mils, and the two formulas that show up on every test in this section.

Wire sizing

AWG & Kcmil

AWG — American Wire Gauge

  • The standard U.S. sizing system for electrical conductors.
  • Runs from 36 AWG (thinnest) to 0000 / 4-0 AWG (thickest). Above that you switch to Kcmil.
  • Smaller diameter = more resistance. Using a smaller wire in a series circuit increases total circuit resistance, which limits current flow.

Kcmil — Thousand Circular Mils

  • Measures the cross-sectional area of a wire, not just its diameter.
  • 1 mil = 0.001 inches. A circular mil is the area of a circle one mil across.
  • 1 Kcmil = 1,000 circular mils.
  • Higher Kcmil = larger area = more current-carrying capacity. A 1/0 AWG wire is 53,480 cmil.

Converting diameter to Kcmil

Wire diameter = 0.5 inches
1. 0.5 × 1,000 = 500 mils
2. 500² = 250,000 circular mils
3. 250,000 ÷ 1,000 = 250 Kcmil
Formula

Voltage Drop

Resistance in a conductor causes voltage drop. That unwanted heat loss in conductors and loads is called I²R loss. Resistance depends on the circular mil area, the length, the material, and the temperature — and is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.

VD = 2 (K × L × I) ÷ CM
KConductor material factor — copper ≈ 10.4, aluminum ≈ 2.4
LLength in feet (one-way distance to the load)
IAmperage applied
CMCircular mils — the wire's cross-sectional area
